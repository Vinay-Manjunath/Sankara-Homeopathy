Diabetes Mellitus


Definition

Diabetes Mellitus is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by elevated levels of blood glucose (sugar) due to either insufficient insulin production or ineffective use of insulin by the body.

Types of Diabetes

Type 1 Diabetes (Insulin-Dependent)

An autoimmune condition where the body destroys insulin-producing cells. Common in children and young adults. Requires lifelong insulin therapy.

Type 2 Diabetes (Non-Insulin Dependent)

Caused by insulin resistance and relative insulin deficiency. Most common in adults. Linked to obesity and lifestyle.

Gestational Diabetes

Occurs during pregnancy and usually resolves after childbirth. Increases future risk of type 2 diabetes.

Other Specific Types

Includes diabetes due to genetic conditions, pancreatic disorders, hormonal imbalances, or medications like steroids.

Symptoms

• Frequent urination (polyuria)
• Excessive thirst (polydipsia)
• Increased hunger (polyphagia)
• Unexplained weight loss
• Fatigue
• Blurred vision
• Slow-healing wounds
• Numbness or tingling in hands or feet
• Recurrent infections

Complications

Acute Complications

• Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)
• Hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state (HHS)
• Hypoglycemia (low blood sugar)

Chronic Complications

• Macrovascular: Heart disease, stroke, peripheral artery disease
• Microvascular: Retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy
• Others: Foot ulcers, infections, sexual dysfunction

Investigations

• Fasting Blood Sugar (FBS)
• Postprandial Blood Sugar (PPBS)
• HbA1c (Glycated Hemoglobin)
• Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT)
• Urine Sugar and Ketones
• Lipid Profile
• Kidney Function Tests (Creatinine, Microalbuminuria)
• Fundoscopy (Eye examination)

Hypertension


Definition

Hypertension, also known as high blood pressure, is a chronic condition where the force of the blood against artery walls is consistently too high, increasing the risk of heart disease, stroke, and other complications.

Types of Hypertension

Primary (Essential) Hypertension

The most common type, develops gradually over years without a known specific cause. Often influenced by lifestyle and genetic factors.

Secondary Hypertension

Caused by an underlying condition such as kidney disease, hormonal disorders, or certain medications. Tends to appear suddenly and may be more severe.

Symptoms

• Often asymptomatic ("silent killer")
• Headaches (especially in the morning)
• Dizziness
• Blurred or double vision
• Nosebleeds
• Shortness of breath
• Palpitations or chest pain (in severe cases)

Complications

Cardiovascular Complications

• Heart attack (myocardial infarction)
• Stroke
• Heart failure
• Aneurysm

Other Complications

• Kidney damage (hypertensive nephropathy)
• Vision loss (hypertensive retinopathy)
• Cognitive impairment or dementia

Investigations

• Blood Pressure Monitoring
• Ambulatory Blood Pressure Monitoring (ABPM)
• Electrocardiogram (ECG)
• Echocardiogram
• Kidney Function Tests
• Lipid Profile
• Urinalysis
• Fundus Examination (to assess eye damage)

Thyroid Disorders (Hypothyroidism / Hyperthyroidism)


Definition

Thyroid disorders are conditions that affect the thyroid gland, a butterfly-shaped gland in the neck that regulates metabolism through hormone production. The two most common types are hypothyroidism (underactive thyroid) and hyperthyroidism (overactive thyroid).

Types of Thyroid Disorders

Hypothyroidism

Occurs when the thyroid gland fails to produce enough thyroid hormones, leading to a slow metabolism. It is more common in women and can result from autoimmune disease, iodine deficiency, or thyroid surgery.

Hyperthyroidism

Occurs when the thyroid gland produces excessive thyroid hormones, accelerating metabolism. It is often caused by Graves’ disease, thyroid nodules, or inflammation of the thyroid.

Symptoms

Symptoms of Hypothyroidism

• Fatigue and sluggishness
• Weight gain
• Cold intolerance
• Dry skin and brittle hair
• Depression
• Constipation
• Slow heart rate
• Menstrual irregularities

Symptoms of Hyperthyroidism

• Weight loss despite normal or increased appetite
• Heat intolerance and sweating
• Nervousness, anxiety, and irritability
• Palpitations or rapid heartbeat
• Tremors in the hands
• Frequent bowel movements
• Bulging eyes (Graves’ disease)

Complications

Complications of Hypothyroidism

• Goiter (enlarged thyroid gland)
• Infertility
• Myxedema (severe hypothyroidism)
• Heart disease
• Depression and cognitive decline

Complications of Hyperthyroidism

• Osteoporosis
• Heart rhythm disorders (e.g., atrial fibrillation)
• Thyroid storm (life-threatening overactivity)
• Eye problems (Graves’ orbitopathy)

Investigations

• TSH (Thyroid Stimulating Hormone)
• Free T3 and Free T4 levels
• Anti-TPO antibodies (for autoimmune thyroiditis)
• TRAb (for Graves’ disease)
• Thyroid ultrasound
• Radioactive iodine uptake test

Polycystic Ovarian Disease (PCOD)


Definition

PCOD is a hormonal disorder in women of reproductive age, characterized by the formation of multiple small cysts in the ovaries, irregular menstrual cycles, and hormonal imbalances, especially elevated androgens.

Types of PCOD

Insulin-Resistant PCOD

Associated with obesity and high insulin levels, leading to increased androgen production. Common in women with metabolic syndrome or type 2 diabetes risk.

Inflammatory PCOD

Chronic low-grade inflammation disrupts ovulation and hormone production, often seen with acne, fatigue, and digestive issues.

Post-Pill PCOD

Occurs in some women after stopping oral contraceptive pills, due to a temporary imbalance in hormones and delayed ovulation return.

Symptoms

• Irregular or absent periods
• Excess facial and body hair (hirsutism)
• Weight gain, especially around the abdomen
• Oily skin and acne
• Hair thinning or hair loss on the scalp
• Difficulty in conceiving (infertility)
• Mood swings, anxiety, or depression
• Fatigue and poor energy levels

Complications

• Infertility or subfertility
• Type 2 diabetes and insulin resistance
• High cholesterol and cardiovascular disease
• Sleep apnea
• Endometrial hyperplasia or cancer
• Obesity and metabolic syndrome

Investigations

• Ultrasound pelvis (to detect ovarian cysts)
• LH and FSH levels (usually raised LH:FSH ratio)
• Total and free testosterone
• DHEA-S levels
• Fasting insulin and glucose levels
• Lipid profile
• TSH and Prolactin (to rule out other causes)

Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA)


Definition

Rheumatoid Arthritis is a chronic autoimmune disorder that primarily affects joints. The immune system mistakenly attacks the synovial membrane, leading to joint inflammation, pain, swelling, and eventual joint damage and deformity.

Types of Rheumatoid Arthritis

Seropositive RA

Most common type, where rheumatoid factor (RF) or anti-CCP antibodies are present in the blood, indicating a more aggressive disease course.

Seronegative RA

Rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP antibodies are absent, but the clinical symptoms mimic seropositive RA. Diagnosis is based on signs, symptoms, and imaging.

Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis (JIA)

Autoimmune arthritis affecting children under 16 years of age, leading to joint inflammation and stiffness.

Symptoms

• Joint pain and stiffness (especially in the morning)
• Swelling in small joints (fingers, wrists, ankles)
• Warmth and redness over joints
• Fatigue and low-grade fever
• Weight loss
• Reduced joint mobility
• Symmetrical joint involvement

Complications

• Joint deformities and disability
• Osteoporosis
• Rheumatoid nodules
• Lung involvement (interstitial lung disease)
• Increased risk of heart disease
• Eye inflammation (scleritis, uveitis)

Investigations

• Rheumatoid Factor (RF)
• Anti-CCP (Cyclic Citrullinated Peptide) antibodies
• ESR (Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate)
• CRP (C-reactive Protein)
• X-ray or MRI of affected joints
• CBC (to check for anemia of chronic disease)

Psoriasis


Definition

Psoriasis is a chronic autoimmune skin condition that speeds up the life cycle of skin cells, causing cells to build up rapidly on the skin surface. This results in scaling, inflammation, redness, and itching. It can affect the skin, scalp, nails, and joints.

Types of Psoriasis

Plaque Psoriasis

The most common type, characterized by raised, red patches covered with silvery-white scales.

Guttate Psoriasis

Appears as small, drop-shaped lesions, often triggered by infections like strep throat.

Inverse Psoriasis

Occurs in skin folds (like armpits, groin), with smooth, shiny, red patches without much scaling.

Pustular Psoriasis

Characterized by white pustules surrounded by red skin, mostly on palms and soles.

Erythrodermic Psoriasis

A severe, life-threatening form causing widespread redness, severe itching, and skin shedding.

Symptoms

• Red, raised patches with silvery-white scales
• Itching, burning, or soreness
• Dry, cracked skin that may bleed
• Thickened, pitted, or ridged nails
• Joint pain (psoriatic arthritis)
• Flare-ups triggered by stress, infections, cold weather, or injury

Complications

• Psoriatic arthritis (joint inflammation)
• Increased risk of cardiovascular disease
• Depression and social anxiety
• Nail damage and deformity
• Impact on quality of life due to visible lesions

Investigations

• Clinical diagnosis based on skin examination
• Skin biopsy (if uncertain diagnosis)
• Blood tests to rule out autoimmune conditions
• Rheumatoid factor and ANA (for psoriatic arthritis)
• X-ray or MRI (for joint involvement)

Eczema (Atopic Dermatitis)


Definition

Eczema is a chronic inflammatory skin condition characterized by red, itchy, and dry skin. It is commonly seen in children but can occur at any age. It is often linked to allergies, asthma, and a family history of atopic conditions.

Types of Eczema

Atopic Dermatitis

The most common form, associated with genetic predisposition, environmental triggers, and immune dysregulation.

Contact Dermatitis

Triggered by skin contact with irritants (irritant contact) or allergens (allergic contact), leading to redness and itching.

Dyshidrotic Eczema

Characterized by small, itchy blisters on palms, soles, and fingers. Triggered by stress, sweat, or allergens.

Seborrheic Dermatitis

Affects oily areas like the scalp, face, and chest. Presents with flaky, greasy scales and redness.

Nummular Eczema

Round or oval-shaped itchy patches, usually on legs, arms, or torso. Often triggered by dry skin or irritation.

Symptoms

• Intense itching
• Red, inflamed skin
• Dryness, scaling, and rough patches
• Oozing or crusting in acute cases
• Skin thickening and darkening in chronic cases
• Burning or stinging sensation
• Sleep disturbances due to itching

Complications

• Secondary bacterial or viral infections
• Skin discoloration or scarring
• Thickened, leathery skin from chronic scratching (lichenification)
• Emotional distress, anxiety, or social withdrawal
• Reduced quality of life, especially in children

Investigations

• Clinical diagnosis based on appearance and history
• Allergy testing (skin prick or IgE test)
• Patch testing for contact dermatitis
• Skin swab (if secondary infection is suspected)

Acne (Pimples)


Definition

Acne is a common skin condition that occurs when hair follicles become clogged with oil (sebum) and dead skin cells. It commonly affects teenagers but can occur at any age. Acne can range from blackheads and whiteheads to painful cysts and nodules.

Types of Acne

Comedonal Acne

Includes blackheads (open comedones) and whiteheads (closed comedones) caused by blocked pores.

Inflammatory Acne

Red, swollen pimples or papules that occur when blocked pores become infected with bacteria.

Cystic Acne

Hormonal Acne

Symptoms

• Recurrent breakouts causing emotional distress

Investigations

Hair Fall (Hair Loss)


Definition

Hair fall refers to excessive shedding or loss of hair from the scalp, which may lead to thinning, bald patches, or complete hair loss. It can be temporary or chronic and is influenced by various internal and external factors.

Types of Hair Fall

Androgenic Alopecia

Also known as male or female pattern baldness. It is hereditary and linked to hormonal sensitivity in hair follicles.

Telogen Effluvium

A temporary shedding of hair triggered by stress, illness, surgery, childbirth, or nutritional deficiencies.

Alopecia Areata

An autoimmune condition where the immune system attacks hair follicles, leading to round bald patches.

Traction Alopecia

Hair loss due to constant pulling or tight hairstyles damaging the hair roots.

Nutritional Hair Fall

Hair loss due to deficiencies in iron, protein, zinc, biotin, or other essential nutrients.

Symptoms

• Excessive hair strands on pillow, comb, or while washing
• Widening of hair parting or thinning of crown
• Receding hairline

• Itchy or flaky scalp (in some cases)

Complications

• Permanent hair loss (in untreated chronic cases)

• Social withdrawal and anxiety

• Trichoscopy (scalp and hair examination)

• Vitamin D and B12 levels

Alopecia (Hair Loss Patches)


Definition

Alopecia refers to partial or complete hair loss from the scalp or other parts of the body. The most common type is Alopecia Areata, an autoimmune condition where the immune system attacks hair follicles, leading to round or oval bald patches.

Types of Alopecia

Alopecia Areata

Characterized by sudden patchy hair loss on the scalp, beard, or other areas. Caused by an autoimmune response against hair follicles.

Alopecia Totalis

Complete loss of hair on the scalp. It may begin as patchy alopecia and progress to total scalp baldness.

Alopecia Universalis

Loss of all body hair, including eyebrows, eyelashes, and pubic hair. It is the most advanced form of alopecia areata.

• Permanent scarring (in cicatricial types)

• Social withdrawal, especially in children and teens

Investigations

• Clinical diagnosis based on appearance
• Dermoscopy or trichoscopy of the scalp

• Thyroid profile (as alopecia is linked to thyroid dysfunction)
• Vitamin D, B12, and iron studies

Definition

Dry Dandruff

Fine, white, dry flakes usually caused by dry scalp or cold weather. Often associated with itching but not inflammation.

Oily Dandruff

• Social embarrassment and self-consciousness

Investigations

• Allergy history or hormonal work-up (if recurrent or chronic)

Cold and Cough in Children


Definition

Cold and cough in children are common upper respiratory tract infections caused by viruses or environmental irritants. Symptoms may include runny nose, sneezing, cough, fever, and throat irritation. These are frequent in early childhood due to developing immunity.

Types

Viral Cold

Most common type caused by rhinovirus or coronavirus. Presents with runny nose, mild fever, sneezing, and watery eyes.

Allergic Cold (Allergic Rhinitis)

Triggered by allergens like dust, pollen, or pet dander. Causes sneezing, nasal congestion, and itchy eyes or nose.

Wet Cough

Productive cough with phlegm or mucus, often seen in viral or bacterial infections.

Dry Cough

Non-productive, irritating cough commonly seen post-infection or due to allergens.

Croup or Barking Cough

A viral infection in infants and toddlers causing hoarse, barking cough and noisy breathing (stridor).

Symptoms

• Mild to moderate fever

Investigations

Allergies in Children


Definition

Types of Allergies

Respiratory Allergies
